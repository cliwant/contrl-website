Imagine an evaluator halfway through a technical volume. Section 3 has blue headings in one font; section 4 switches to green headings in another. The page numbers move from the bottom right to the bottom center. One table runs past the margin. Nothing in the content is wrong, yet the evaluator starts to wonder whether section 4 was written by a different company, and whether this team will deliver as unevenly as it formats.

Page and document design is the work that makes a proposal look like one compliant, consistent document, so evaluators can find, read and trust what it says. The APMP Body of Knowledge treats a proposal's visual presentation as nearly as important as its content. Its central tool is a document template: built early, compliant with the customer's rules, shared with everyone who writes or draws, and applied to every page. Layout strategies, a planned production step and enough scheduled time do the rest.

This series works through the APMP Body of Knowledge as summarized in the APMP Foundation Study Guide. Episode 18 covered headings and episode 19 covered graphics. This episode covers the page they both sit on, and the document that holds all the pages together.

What page design has to achieve

The Study Guide frames the question simply: when evaluators finish your document, are they interested and persuaded, or bored and unimpressed? Layout alone won't decide that, but poor layout can tip a reader toward the second answer before the argument has a chance. The guide sets four requirements for every proposal document.

A proposal document mustWhat that means in practice
Follow instructionsObey the customer's formatting rules, your company's guidelines and the proposal outline
Look professional, appropriatelyAppealing in a way that suits the customer and the subject, not flashy for its own sake
Highlight what mattersUse design techniques to draw the eye to specific text and information
Be produced efficientlyAchievable with the people, software and time actually available

All four depend on decisions made before writing starts: colors, fonts, page size, how the logo is used and similar details. The guide's advice is to capture those choices in a clear document template and communicate it to the whole team, so that every contribution arrives compliant and consistent instead of being fixed at the end.

Who owns the page: the desktop publisher

In teams large enough to have one, the person responsible for this is the desktop publisher. The role's core job is to create the document template, make sure it follows the instructions, and apply it evenly across every page and every file in the submission. Around that core, the Study Guide lists several related tasks:

  • Inserting graphics into the document, labeling them and numbering them, often in sequence or by section.
  • Building a list of figures and tables.
  • Working with writers, editors and graphic designers so the style stays the same across everyone's work.
  • Handling the link to physical production: printing, binding and the creation of electronic files.

Smaller teams rarely have a dedicated desktop publisher, and someone on the proposal team does this work alongside other tasks. The responsibilities don't disappear when the job title does, so it helps to name who owns them.

Choose the right tools, and the same tools

A desktop publisher should know the software most used in their industry and keep up with new versions and features. Vendors and third parties offer training, and plenty of it is free online. The point the guide stresses most is practical: a template can be built in many programs, but it works best when every team member uses the same program in the same version. A template that renders one way on one laptop and another way on the next is not a template.

The document template: compliant first, then consistent

The template has two duties, in a fixed order.

First, compliance. The template must follow every formatting instruction the customer gives. The customer may set the paper size, the margins, the maximum page count and the font; these are not suggestions. Where the customer is silent, use your company's branding or style guide. Where there is no guide, follow what is most common in your industry. That gives a simple order of precedence:

  1. The customer's instructions in the RFP.
  2. Your corporate branding and style guidelines.
  3. The usual standards in your industry.

Compliance here is the same idea episode 1 applied to content: missing a formatting rule can cost you before anyone reads your solution.

Second, consistency. Consistency shows your level of professionalism, and it also carries meaning. Readers assume that when structure, color, font, imagery or layout visibly changes, something about the content has changed too. If the change was accidental and nothing explains it, the bidder looks unprepared or careless. A consistent document lets the evaluator stop noticing the format and pay attention to the argument.

What the template defines

The Study Guide lists the main elements a document template should settle. The list is a minimum; add whatever your proposals need.

ElementWhat to decide
Final productionPrinted and bound, or delivered electronically? This drives page size and orientation and may limit how many or what kind of graphics you can use
Page marginsWidth on all four sides; may vary with binding (a wider inside margin) or orientation
ColumnsA single column is the usual choice and the simplest to work with. Two or more add visual interest and fit slightly more words per page, but take more skill to set up with tables, callout boxes and graphics
Headers and footersOn every page, so the evaluator always knows which document, section and page they are on; may include the company logo, proposal name and submission date
FontsSet through the software's styles, for each type of text (see below)
ColorsUsed in header and footer lines, tables, headings and other text; drawn from the customer, your brand, or a mix of both
Graphic stylesHow graphics sit with the text: within the margins or columns, at the right resolution for print or screen, placed after the text that mentions them, each with an action caption
Special section templatesLayouts for parts that repeat a structure, such as resumes, Q&A sections, the executive summary or past performance

On graphics, the guide adds a production rule worth repeating: if a graphic was made in a separate program, don't edit or resize it inside the publishing software. Fix it at the source and re-import it. (The action caption itself was the subject of episode 19.)

Fonts and the five kinds of text

Beyond choosing a typeface, the template should say how to treat each kind of text on the page:

Kind of textWhat to define
Body textThe default style for most of the document
HeadingsHow they stand out from body text: another font, a color, outline numbering (see episode 18)
Bulleted listsThe glyph used for each level: a dot, a square, a dash
EmphasisBold is usually the best way to stress a short word or phrase on a text page; underline and italics are alternatives
Callouts and testimonialsA color, a box or another element that sets them apart

If the RFP or your style guide names a font, use it. If neither does, pick a widely used, readable typeface; the guide's examples are Arial, Calibri, Cambria and Times New Roman. Keep body text at 10 to 12 points or larger; text inside graphics and tables can go down to 8 points.

Tables for recurring structures

The guide recommends tables for information evaluators look up repeatedly, such as project names, dates or job titles, and for paired ideas that recur across a bid: question and answer, feature and benefit, your company and a competitor. A table style defined once in the template can be reused in every volume. The feature-benefit pairing will look familiar from episode 9.

Fit the template to the team, and run it as a process

A template is only useful if the team can actually produce it. The Study Guide says to design it within three limits: the desktop publisher's skill, the software available and the time available. A three-column layout with wrapped graphics is not an improvement if nobody can build it by the deadline.

The template also needs a process around it. The desktop publisher should keep a consistent file structure and version control, and the team should know how documents are handed in for formatting, how they come back, and how to apply the template themselves. Write those steps down and share them, the same way you share the template.

Prepare for production

The last step turns a working document into something the customer receives: a print-ready file, an electronic submission, or both. Outside native word-processing formats, the most common format for both is the PDF.

Creating a PDF is effectively printing the document to a file. That has two useful consequences. It can save printing costs, and it locks the layout so that text, graphics and tables can't easily be changed. Your pages then appear as you intended, without page breaks that shifted on another computer, colors that came out wrong or a font quietly replaced by another. Those are exactly the errors that can turn a compliant document into a non-compliant one at the last minute.

PDFs help in two more ways:

  • File size. A PDF can be compressed as a whole, without reworking every graphic, to meet upload or email limits.
  • Advanced features. Full PDF software can add security settings, fillable forms, multimedia and hyperlinks within and between documents. These need the full version of Adobe Acrobat; the free Acrobat Reader is what has made PDF the standard way to exchange documents.

Production also feeds back into design. If the proposal will be printed and bound, the template needs a binding margin and print-resolution graphics. If it goes only to screens, links and color matter more. That is why final production is the first item in the template, not the last.

Six layout strategies

Once the template is set, the layout of each page still needs judgment. The Study Guide names six strategies and notes that they work best together. The right-hand column is my own suggestion for a proposal page.

StrategyWhat it doesOn a proposal page (example)
ContrastCreates emphasis through opposite pairs (big and small, black and white, square and round), and can bring information forward or push it backOne large figure for the customer's main benefit; supporting numbers small beside it
ProximityItems placed close together read as relatedKeep a graphic next to the paragraph that explains it, and a proof point next to the claim it supports
White spaceGives readers a break; has to be traded against page limitsUnder a tight page limit, cut words before you cut all the space around headings
ConsistencyThe same fonts, colors and graphic and table styles help evaluators navigate and signal levels of informationEvery requirement response uses the same table style, so evaluators learn where to look
BalanceNo part of the page feels heavier than the rest; columns, graphics and tables can be arranged to achieve itDon't stack every graphic in the left column of a two-column page
AlignmentHow text, headings, graphics and tables line up affects how easy the page is to read and how the content is perceivedAlign graphics, tables and callouts to the same column edges on every page

Balance has an interesting side note in the guide: layout can hint at a company's character. A company selling innovation might choose a more dynamic, deliberately uneven layout, while an established firm might choose a calm, symmetrical one that supports its claims of stability. Either is fine if it is a decision, not an accident.

Schedule it and train for it

Formatting is real work, and it is easy to squeeze when the writing runs late. The Study Guide's summary makes two points about managing it.

  • Schedule formatting and desktop publishing time using metrics, and plan it around the major reviews. If you know roughly how long your team takes to format a page or a section, you can book that time instead of guessing.
  • Train the team and keep up with software changes. Having a program installed is not the same as knowing how to use it, and a writer who fights the template's styles creates formatting work for someone else.

The same summary asks the whole team, not only the publisher, to understand that consistent page design improves readability and understanding. When writers see the template as part of the message, they stop working around it.

Common mistakes

  1. Building the template late. If writers start in their own files and formats, someone has to reconcile everything during the final days, which is when errors slip in.
  2. Treating customer rules as defaults. Page size, margins, page limits and fonts in the RFP are requirements, not starting points for adjustment.
  3. Accidental variation. A change in heading color or table style between sections looks like a change in meaning or, worse, like carelessness.
  4. A template the team can't produce. An ambitious layout that exceeds the publisher's skill, the software or the schedule is worse than a plain one done well.
  5. Editing graphics in the wrong place. Resizing a graphic inside the publishing software instead of its source program degrades it and breaks consistency.
  6. Mixed software versions. The same file opened in different versions can reflow pages and shift page breaks.
  7. Leaving no time for production. PDF conversion, file-size checks and printing take time and can expose errors that need fixing.

Checklist for your next proposal

  1. Collect every formatting rule in the RFP (page size, margins, page limit, font, file format, size limits) before designing anything.
  2. Decide final production first: print, electronic or both, and what that means for size, orientation and graphics.
  3. Build the document template in order of precedence: customer rules, then brand guidelines, then industry norms.
  4. Define every text style: body, headings, bullets, emphasis (bold first) and callouts, with a legible font of 10 to 12 points or larger.
  5. Set graphic and table rules: within margins, right resolution, after first mention, action caption, and special layouts for recurring sections.
  6. Check the template fits the publisher's skill, the software and the time available.
  7. Put everyone on the same software and version, and hand out the template and the file-handling process at kickoff.
  8. Review layout with the six strategies: contrast, proximity, white space, consistency, balance, alignment.
  9. Schedule formatting and production time around the major reviews, and leave time to create, check and compress the final PDF.
